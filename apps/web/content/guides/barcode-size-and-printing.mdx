A retail barcode (UPC-A or EAN-13) is about 37 mm wide at its standard size, and you can print it anywhere from 80% to 200% of that. Label barcodes like Code 128 have no fixed size: keep the narrowest bar at least 0.19 mm wide, and wider for scanning at a distance. Whatever the type, keep the blank margins, use dark bars on a light background, and send your printer a file that keeps every bar sharp.

## Retail barcodes: the standard sizes

| Type                                | Width at 100% with margins    | Allowed range                                    |
| ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| [UPC-A](/barcode-generator/upc)     | 37.3 mm                       | 80% to 200% (about 30 to 75 mm)                  |
| [EAN-13](/barcode-generator/ean-13) | 37.3 mm                       | 80% to 200%                                      |
| [EAN-8](/barcode-generator/ean-8)   | 26.7 mm                       | 80% to 200%                                      |
| [UPC-E](/barcode-generator/upc-e)   | 22.1 mm                       | 80% to 200%                                      |
| [ITF-14](/barcode-generator/itf-14) | About 152 mm with bearer bars | About 50% to 100%, depending on the print method |

Our generator starts each retail type at 100% and tells you the size as you change it. Bigger is easier to scan; going below 80% is the most common reason a retail barcode fails at the till.

## Label barcodes: bar width matters

For [Code 128](/barcode-generator/code-128), [Code 39](/barcode-generator/code-39) and [GS1-128](/barcode-generator/gs1-128), the size comes from the narrowest bar (the X-dimension) and how much data you encode.

- **0.19 mm:** the practical minimum, for close-range scanning on good printers.
- **0.25 to 0.33 mm:** a safe choice for most labels.
- **0.5 mm and up:** for warehouse labels scanned from a distance.

The generator shows the narrowest bar as you resize and warns when it gets too thin. To make a long code narrower, shorten the data rather than shrinking the bars.

For 2D codes ([Data Matrix](/barcode-generator/data-matrix), [Aztec](/barcode-generator/aztec), [PDF417](/barcode-generator/pdf417)), the same idea applies to each square: 0.25 mm is a sensible minimum for scanners, more for phones.

## Margins: don’t trim them

Every barcode needs blank space on both sides, called the quiet zone. Scanners use it to find where the code starts and ends. A UPC-A needs about 9 bar widths on each side; Code 128 needs at least 10. Our downloads include the margins: don’t crop them, and don’t let a box edge, text or a design element run into them.

## Colours and backgrounds

- **Dark bars, light background.** Black on white is best. Dark blue, green or brown on white, cream or light grey also work.
- **Avoid red, orange and yellow bars.** Many scanners use red light, so red bars look like background.
- **No reversed barcodes.** Light bars on a dark background fail on many scanners.
- **Transparent backgrounds** are fine only if what’s behind is plain and light.

## Which file to send your printer

- **PNG at the final size:** for office printers, labels and most digital printing. Every bar is a whole number of pixels, so widths stay exact. Don’t resize it afterwards.
- **SVG, PDF or EPS:** for packaging and print shops. Vector files stay sharp at any size and let the printer adjust bar width for ink spread. These come with a paid plan.
- **Thermal printers:** print at 100% at the printer’s own resolution; resized images print uneven bars. Zebra users can use ZPL.

## Before the print run

1. Print one proof at the final size on the real material.
2. Scan it with the kind of scanner that will read it: a till, a warehouse gun or a phone app.
3. Check the number it reads matches your records.
4. Look at curved or shiny surfaces: bars running around a bottle should run up and down, not around.
